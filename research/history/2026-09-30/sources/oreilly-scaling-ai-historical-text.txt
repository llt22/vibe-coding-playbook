If you’re a CTO, I have good and bad news for you. The bad news is that many organizations are getting no ROI from adopting AI within their engineering orgs (according to the 2025 NANDA and METR studies). The good news is that other companies
are seeing a tremendous acceleration in their ability to deliver value. The best news is
that the differences between the high- and low-performing orgs are now becoming
clearer–and so is how to move from one category to the other.
If you are a software engineering leader (especially if you’re running an org of 100+)
and you’re looking for good practices for adopting and scaling AI to accelerate how
your org delivers business value, this book was written for you. The synthesis of hundreds of interviews with “CTOs at Scale” and executives from top AI companies, it is
designed to provide you with a comprehensive plan for experimenting with and scaling the adoption of GenAI across your org. Recognizing that the hardest parts of
developing software effectively are almost always about humans and very seldom
about code, it focuses on the strategies, stakeholder engagement, and change management you’ll need to lead your org through turbulent times.
Here are some things this book is not focused on:

Preface

In Chapter 1, I tell a “tale of two orgs,” synthesizing hundreds of interviews into a
vignette of two fictitious companies (Flatline Systems and Summit SaaS) that epitomize the two extremes between which most real companies fall when adopting AI for
engineering. I then touch on some key takeaways that I’ll unpack in more detail
throughout the rest of the book. In Chapter 2, I expand on the concept of AI as an
amplifier, highlighting some of the organizational traits that maximize the value available by adopting LLMs.
In Chapter 3, I take a step back so we can examine your broader business context.
Most engineering orgs can get value from LLMs, but some face a more urgent, existential threat from AI than others. If you’re selling website-building SaaS software,
you’d better be one of the innovators in adopting AI. If you manage ski resorts, the
risk of GenAI is less existential–whatever happens with LLMs, people are probably
still going to be skiing 10 years from now. I provide a framework for thinking about
AI adoption and “picking a lane” for your company, whether that’s as an innovator or
as a more cautious “late majority” adopter.
In Chapter 4, now that we have clarity on how aggressively your organization wants
to adopt GenAI for software development, I share some good practices for aligning
with the security, audit, risk and legal teams to ensure you are capable of approving
experiments and unblocking your team.
WIth that framework in place, in Part 2 I take some time to envision what engineering orgs might look like in the future. I share what’s working now and possible
futures for the entire SDLC, from specification through coding, reviewing, testing,
and deploying new features. I also look at what’s working for all of the supporting
activities that go on within engineering orgs, from management to hiring to onboarding.
Finally, in Part 3, I provide detailed guidance on the practical steps required to build
out an AI-powered engineering org effectively: from executive alignment, managing
stakeholder expectations, and setting employee expectations to building personal
competence, staffing the initiative, partnering with product, tool selection and approval, identifying experiments, driving adoption, and leading in uncertain times.

A Tale of Two Orgs

Some companies are getting transformative results from large language models
(LLMs); others are just wasting their money and frustrating their engineers. That’s the
GenAI divide. In this chapter, to highlight what’s at stake, I’ve synthesized dozens of
conversations with CTOs into two composite stories so we can compare and contrast
two approaches to engaging with AI in the software development lifecycle (SDLC). I
then pull out some high-level insights from the CTOs who are getting the best results
from their AI transformations.
There is no element of these composites that I’ve not encountered in the wild.

Flatline Systems
Flatline Systems is a publicly traded AdTech company with over 800 engineers, a
third of whom are contractors from a large outsourcing firm. Flatline builds lowlatency, high-performance ad servers for connecting publishers to advertising opportunities in real time. Its code is mainly a collection of Java microservices, with C++

1

for much of the ad-server code. It has most of its apps running on public clouds (with
just a few on-prem holdouts). Test coverage is spotty, manual QA is required for
every release, and deployment of many of the apps still isn’t automated.
The CTO at Flatline knew he needed to show decisive action on GenAI. As soon as it
was announced, he inked a deal to provide Enterprise Copilot access to an initial test
group of 20 engineers to see how much value it could deliver.
The CTO was already overwhelmed with his existing roadmap, but he asked one of
his newest directors to shepherd the pilot and to report back each quarter. He
couldn’t pull the director off of her main project, which was mission-critical, but he
added a sixth quarterly goal to make sure she’d be motivated to make the pilot a success.
At first, the director didn’t seem to understand the assignment. She wanted to completely restructure the pilot, pick teams rather than individuals, and go through a
complicated opportunity-identification process. It made sense, but the CTO had
already provisioned the licenses and had more urgent priorities.
The director kept on asking for more support. She wanted access to Claude and Cursor, even though she already had the Copilot licenses. She wanted to trial additional
niche tools for test generation, code review, and DevOps automation. Most of the
companies she wanted to work with were early-stage venture-backed companies with
very little track record. It wasn’t even worth taking it to the legal team or the CISO–
they’d never sign off on it.
She asked for a budget for onsite training, found some great trainers, and obtained
quotes, but it was really hard to justify spending tens of thousands of dollars on training when the internet is full of blog posts and videos on how to use LLMs.
She also asked if the engineers could get extra time to deliver their projects. It really
made no sense. The whole purpose of the experiment was to speed up the dev team,
not to slow them down.
The worst part was that while some of the engineers seemed to enjoy using Copilot,
according to their managers, there was no obvious improvement in velocity or code
quality. This result was subjective, of course, because it’s really hard to measure developer productivity and the CTO could never justify the cost of implementing
developer-productivity tools. However, it seemed consistent with reports suggesting
that many companies get no ROI from adopting LLMs.
He didn’t fire the director for messing up the pilot, but he made it clear that she was
on probation and had better impress him with her other initiatives if she wanted a
satisfactory rating. She ended up leaving the company shortly afterwards. Clearly he’d
made another poor hiring decision.

The CTO didn’t want to be seen as behind the curve, so despite the lack of proven
value, he decided to allow engineers to request a Copilot license–as long as they could
commit to at least a 10% productivity increase (to be evaluated by their team lead).
Strangely, nobody seemed to care enough about AI to sign up for the licenses. In
addition, when he looked at his regrettable attrition statistics later in the year, he
noticed that a disproportionately high percentage of engineers from the original AI
pilot had left the company. Another downside not enough people were talking about!
The CTO was always a little nervous when new case studies came out showing dramatic improvements at other orgs that were leveraging LLMs. He asked a couple of
distinguished engineers he respected what was up. They explained that while LLMs
are great for startups, they just don’t work at enterprise scale and with legacy code,
especially in niche domains: “The training data just isn’t there. Autocomplete is never
going to replace experienced engineers solving hard problems.”
The CTO still felt like he might be missing something, but the data seemed clear, and
he really needed to focus on his Q4 deliverables to support two big new partnerships
the sales org had brought in. He figured he could always check in again in six months
to see if it was worth revisiting his AI strategy.

Summit SaaS
The engineering org at Summit SaaS had a lot going for it even before it started to
experiment with LLMs. It was a series C, venture-backed dev tool company that
seemed to attract the best engineers, both at its San Francisco headquarters and
across the US and Europe. They had a motivated, remote-first org of 180 engineers.
Summit paid 85% of top of market in San Francisco, and compensation was independent of geography, so they were able to get some particularly capable engineers
outside of the Bay Area.
Summit had a mature platform team of 12 responsible for delivering the tooling
required for the feature teams to ship faster. It was one of the first companies to
rename its platform team a “DevEx” team, to reflect the team’s commitment to optimizing the developer experience for their customers (the engineers).
The DevEx team members weren’t religious about Test-Driven Development, but
they had about 90% code coverage, ran continuous integration (CI) on all branches,
used red/green automated continuous deployment (CD), and leveraged a mature
feature-flagging system for running experiments in production. They also had excellent observability, monitoring, and notifications for system, application, and business
metrics.
The CTO believed deeply in the potential of LLMs and was an early adopter of LLMs
personally, often creating her own prompts to help with communications and planning. She created a lightweight set of AI agents for ingesting data from systems of

and organize regular comms and activities to help the team to share what was working.
They also provided their entire engineering org with access to multiple generalpurpose coding tools for both augmented (IDE-based) and agentic engineering. Over
the next 18 months, they also ran experiments with niche tools for performing code
and security reviews, automating DevOps requests, modernizing some of their older
apps, and generating tests and documentation. They experimented with multiple
tools in each category and allowed different teams to run experiments in parallel.
The CTO made it clear that they were going to be an AI-forward engineering org,
and that a 10 to 15% decline in short-term productivity was acceptable–providing
that it accelerated AI learning and adoption across the engineering org. During Summit’s annual hack week, she asked everyone to focus on leveraging AI in their submissions–including the business ops teams, who vibe-coded some amazing prototypes
for backend reporting and some customer-requested features. She also encouraged
her teams to take a little slack time to play with LLMs more intensely.

She spoke frequently at conferences about AI adoption and retooled her hiring process to require engineers to use LLMs in the interview process. She wanted to attract
the engineers who were most excited about adopting AI in their work.
A number of the experimental projects failed, and she noticed slightly higher levels of
regrettable attrition than usual. In exit interviews, the engineers often said that they
didn’t want to become “engineering managers to a bunch of agents.” But her AI adoption team coalesced around a few successful tools that they rolled out across the org.
They also started to build out a company-wide context repository to hold everything:
business goals, go-to-market strategies, customer-service conversations, engineering
experiments, and automatically generated documentation for the app’s capabilities.
In partnership with the product org, the team also started to experiment with agents
for streamlining the process of going from objectives and key results (OKRs) to
experiments to product requirements documents (PRDs) to tickets to code. Often the
agents would get carried away, but over time they found that 30 to 40% of their app’s
features could be coded agentically, with a human reviewing and cleaning up that
code. The rest could be accelerated by 30 to 80% by leveraging augmented engineering within an IDE. They also started to get much more rigorous about the format of
PRDs and started committing them to a GitHub repo. They noticed a direct correlation between the specificity of their PRDs and the quality of the resulting agentically
generated code.
They also experimented with using LLMs to track engineer engagement, support 1:1
meetings, bring more data to performance reviews, and automate a lot of the project
management across the company. They still had a great Technical Project Manager,
but her role evolved from administrative to consultative, allowing her to focus much
more on alignment and prioritization and much less on reporting and documentation.
The CTO was clear that “LLMs right now are as bad as they are ever going to be” and
that they still had a long way to go in learning how to use them effectively. But with
some clear wins, broad adoption, and a team excited to leverage the latest tooling, she
was excited to continue trialing the latest tools, with the goal of maximizing her
team’s ability to deliver business value in a relaxed but productive manner.

Where is your org?
Most companies’ AI adoption is somewhere in between Flatline Systems and Summit
SaaS, but the two extremes help to highlight some key commonalities in companies
that are getting the best ROI from their GenAI initiatives.
AI isn’t something you can just read about or delegate. If you’re not personally using
AI daily, you won’t be able to lead your org successfully through your AI transition.
You should be experimenting with LLM-based tools for presentations (like

AI requires investment. Change management is a full-time job that requires funding
and support, and your team may be a little less productive as they build the skills

You’re going to need an executive with platform/DevOps experience within the engineering org who is focused on driving your AI experimentation and adoption. They
should prioritize enabling an efficient flow of work from specification to production,
and they should have enough experience and credibility within the org that they’ll be
able to get everyone–from individual contributors to executives (think the CISO and
the audit and legal teams)–to engage with their proposals.
You also need to think about incentives. Saying “I need a proven ROI from every
token used” creates very different incentives than saying “I’ll promote the people who
are burning the most tokens.” In the early experimentation phase, the latter will accelerate your learning–even though not every experiment will prove fruitful.
You need to get out of your team’s way. If you don’t allow them to experiment with all
the tools that are safe to try, they’ll either hide their usage (“shadow AI”) or move to a
company that allows them to run experiments more quickly and with less bureaucracy.
You need to rethink hiring, too. Using AI isn’t cheating: it’s the job. As a CTO, the way
you position AI use in the hiring process sends a very clear signal about the kind of
company you want to be. Do you want to attract or deter engineers who are excited to
augment their capabilities with LLMs?
Most importantly, you need to focus on the fundamentals. It turns out that AI is an
amplifier; the more efficiently your engineering org operates without LLMs, the more
value they will get from adopting them.

The teams that are getting the greatest value out of AI are the ones that already have
the strongest culture, the clearest customer focus, the most robust engineering practices, and the slickest developer experience. In this chapter I highlight some of the consistent elements I’ve seen in the companies getting the best ROI from adopting LLMs.
In Part 3 of the book, “Building the AI-Powered Engineering Org,” I’ll dig a lot more
deeply into identifying your organizational maturity in each of these areas and
improving your processes and practices. Think of this as a high-level cheat sheet for
reviewing how ready your organization is for AI-based acceleration.

Culture and Trust
AI adoption is much easier when you have a trusting relationship with your team.
Change is scary–especially when it’s not your idea. AI is likely to transform the composition and nature of roles across product and engineering. Without a supportive
culture, your team is unlikely to take the risks necessary to succeed with AI.

7

None of us knows exactly how AI is going to impact the nature of programming jobs.
You may not be able to make promises about what the future will hold. But if your
team knows that they can trust you to be honest and transparent and that you care
about their wellbeing and careers, they are much more likely to feel safe when experimenting with new ways of working.
The nature of experiments is that many of them don’t work out. We’re still learning
the right way to leverage LLMs to accelerate the SDLC. It’s important to decouple the
success of experiments from performance reviews, bonuses, and career development.
If you don’t, nobody will be willing to risk taking on experimental projects.
While it’s important that you provide a strong mandate for adopting AI, you also
need to work hard to solicit tactical advice and experience from the people who are
actually using the tools. Good practices for leveraging AI are not intuitive and are
most often discovered through hands-on experimentation. It’s the individual contributors who are most likely to identify how to get the most value out of LLMs. Make it
safe for them to tell you when your plans don’t make sense, so you can course-correct
quickly.
AI is moving so fast that keeping up with it requires a lot of passion and commitment. I’m not talking about a “996” culture, where you ask your team to work 12
hours a day (9am to 9pm), 6 days a week. But when they are at work, they need to
bring their curiosity, their love of learning, and a commitment to continual experimentation and improvement.
The value that LLMs can provide is directly correlated to the comprehensiveness and
accuracy of the context available to them. More than ever, it’s becoming critical to
avoid relying on “water-cooler conversations” or even emails and private DMs for
communication, and to instead use transparent, persistent communication mechanisms like wikis and public Slack channels for any information that is not fundamentally sensitive. It’s the only way that all of your teammates–human and agentic–can
know what’s going on.

Customer-Centered Collaboration
The goal of the engineering org is to solve customer problems creatively. Classic Lean
and Agile patterns are incredibly important when it comes to leveraging LLMs to that
end.
Increasingly, the value of an engineer will come from their ability to specify solutions
to business problems that are rigorous, architecturally sound, and maintainable.
There will be far fewer “code monkey” roles, where engineers are simply tasked with
implementing a spec and don’t need to care about the business context.

You’ll need small, self-sufficient teams capable of specifying, designing, developing,
testing, and deploying features to production. If you don’t have those, any improvements in coding time will be dwarfed by the queueing time incurred by coordinating
schedules across multiple teams.
The smaller your pull requests and the less context they require, the less likely it is
that your LLMs will start hallucinating API endpoints and functions that don’t exist.
Small, customer-valued slices of work allow for easier review, greater velocity, and
quicker learning as you see which features actually move the needle on your business
goals.

Engineering Excellence
The more rigorous your engineering practices are, the more you’ll be able to trust
your LLMs to generate code.
The great thing about Test-Driven Development is that it’s a design methodology, not
just a testing strategy. It forces you to think through even the simplest possible behavioral changes you want to make–and to define success before you start implementing
those changes. That is also a great pattern to use with LLMs to make sure you’re
aligned on the outcomes before they start to work on an implementation. While it
doesn’t need to be used for all new features, TDD can be a great methodology for
pairing with an LLM. You and the LLM agree on exactly what behaviors you want to
provide and then you validate that it’s delivering them.
LLMs will make mistakes (as will humans). But if you provide them a quick, automated method to validate success (that is, running a test suite), they’re much more likely
to be able to self-correct without intervention. It’s also much easier to catch regressions when you have good test coverage and an easy way to fire off alerts and report
on any errors encountered.
LLMs can accelerate the delivery of business value. Couple that speed with a focus on
small, thinly sliced user stories, and it’s likely that, as you adopt AI, you will be
deploying to production much more frequently. It’s important to automate your CD
pipeline to keep up with the accelerating pace of feature delivery.
Everyone is testing in production, too, whether it’s testing with real-world load or
testing the edge cases that only come up with real users and real data. It’s important to
have robust feature-flagging tooling to decouple deployment from release. This
allows you to roll out new features to users incrementally to ensure that they’re working as expected.
It’s always been important to have system, application, and business-level reporting
and notifications, so that if a CPU is spiking, an application is timing out, or your
number of active users drops by 50% in a minute, your team gets notified and can

It’s critical to be thoughtful about the developer experience, both individually and in

to support their engineers in adopting LLMs successfully.
The great thing about having a platform team is that it gives you an obvious leverage

already in the business of accelerating your feature teams’ work, so LLMs just become
one more tool they can use to help their customers (your feature teams) go faster.
Having a DevEx team is about more than just rebranding your platform org. It’s a
clear signal that the job of a platform team is to facilitate the feature teams’ experience
of developing software. The engineers are the platform team’s customers, and maintaining a customer focus really helps the platform team to be thoughtful when proposing, implementing, and maintaining tooling for developers–whether that’s a new
internal developer portal (IDP) or a set of agentic coding tools.
Measurement is another key element of engineering success. If you want to improve
your ability to deliver business value quickly without burning out your team, you’ll
need to invest in engineering efficiency metrics so you have a baseline for comparison–whether that’s DORA, SPACE, DX Core 4, or your own unique mix.
AI is not a silver bullet or a quick win, but it can both create an impetus for change
and accelerate the process of adopting new processes and practices.

Change, not tools
If you take just one thing away from this book, it should be that AI adoption is a
change-management problem, not a tooling-selection problem. AI is going to fundamentally change the nature of your engineering organization. It’s not like moving to
the cloud or adopting a developer portal–in fact, the closest analogy might be managing through an acquisition.

You’re getting acquired!
When a company is acquired, both the tech stack and the engineering org are audited
and lots of hard decisions need to be made. Is this the right structure? Are we duplicating functions? Who do we keep in the same role and who do we promote, move,
or let go?
To say the quiet part out loud: we’re going to have to make all of those decisions over the
next few years as AI transforms the SDLC.
Some of this is simply a continuation of trends that were already in play. Ever since
Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg introduced us to “the year of efficiency” in 2023, engineering
orgs have seen a thinning of middle management, a focus on “player coaches,” an
expanding span of control, and higher performance demands on everyone.
There is still a lot of uncertainty and it’s too soon to be making big moves, but your
team knows that eventually, if LLMs fulfill their promise of greater efficiency, you’re
going to have to choose between an accelerated roadmap or a lower headcount. It’s
not obvious which you’ll end up going with.

Creating a change-management strategy
AI tooling is new, but change management has been around for a long time, and
there are classic techniques that can help you to steady and support your org and create an environment where people can share and adopt good practices effectively.
A commitment to change
You need to make it clear that you are already committed to this transformation.
The goal of starting with experiments is to identify how to proceed with LLMs,
not whether to do so.
Transparent communications
Be honest that you don’t know how this will impact the size or the structure of
the org, but that you’re committed to helping your team to build the skills that
will allow them to thrive as augmented engineers, at your company or (eventually) somewhere else.
Identify and empower champions
Empower members of your team to experiment with AI tooling and support
them in doing so. Make sure you have someone responsible for quickly unblocking any obstacles to running your AI experiments.
Listen to the team
As part of your commitment to psychological safety, make sure there are channels where the engineers can be honest about what is and isn’t working with their
experiments, so you can understand what’s happening and course-correct
quickly.

Assess your engineering maturity
It’s important to set realistic expectations about the current maturity of your engineering org and the impact that will have on your ability to leverage AI. If you still
rely on manual QA and deployments and don’t score well on developer efficiency
metrics like DORA, SPACE, or DX Core 4, it doesn’t matter how quickly AI can generate new features–the bottlenecks will develop in reviewing, QAing, and deploying

There is a direct correlation between the maturity of your engineering processes and

much deeper in part 3, but it’s important to realize that if you don’t already have an
efficient, modern SDLC, you’ll have to fix that before you can really accelerate software development using AI. The only good news is that GenAI will be able to help
you accelerate the process of modernizing your engineering org.

Picking a Lane
As a company, the most important strategic decision you have to make is how aggressively you should invest in adopting AI. AI adoption is not free: it will cost money,
time, energy, and focus. Done right, it will provide a substantial return, but it’s important to be realistic about the scope of the investment your company is willing to make
and honest about the rate at which your company will be capable of adopting AI.
Should your company be an innovator, early adopter, early or late majority, or laggard
in terms of adopting AI solutions? There are good reasons to pick many of those
strategies, but the most important thing is for your executive team to discuss, align
on, and then to pick a lane. Then, as an organization, you need to understand the
implications of the choice, own them, and support them appropriately.
The one thing you must do is be consistent. If your CEO is trumpeting your investments in AI and your AI-forward stance while your CISO and audit, risk, and legal
teams are blocking or slow-rolling all of your proposed AI experiments, you will end
up making nobody happy–and the inconsistencies will show up pretty quickly in the
results your org is delivering.

Your “risk team”
Depending on your company, there are some combination of teams, such as
legal, compliance and/or audit groups, whose primary job is to stop you from
doing something dangerous. Unfortunately, experimentation is inherently risky.
It’s important to engage with your risk team about the implications of your AI
adoption strategy.
If you’re planning on being late majority/laggards, it’ll be a pretty easy conversation.
If you want to be innovators or early adopters, you’re going to need to have hard conversations about minimal red lines and guaranteed approval times for new tools that
are measured in days, not weeks.

Your talent team
Your adoption strategy for AI is going to materially impact your recruiting process.
For example, if you want to be an innovator, you’ll need to retool your hiring rubrics
to attract and filter for AI augmented engineers.

Your Engineering org
It’s important to lay out realistic expectations for and with your engineering org. If
you require that no human read or write code within a quarter, that’s a very different
mission from suggesting that people “play with AI a bit” while waiting for the tooling
from your favored vendor to evolve.

Innovator
If you want to be on the bleeding edge to capture the potential upside and/or protect
against material business disruption risks, you’re going to have to push hard on AI

adoption and accept some risks and uncertainty as the cost of moving fast and
(potentially) breaking some things.

Tool Approval
Your approval process needs to be measured in days, not weeks. If your risk team
can’t turn around a review in 3 to 5 business days, that’s going to be the bottleneck
that determines your actual pace of adoption, regardless of what your strategy says.
Red lines should be minimal and well-defined: a short, explicit list of things you won’t

Low-cost experiments (under some agreed threshold) shouldn’t require ROI justification upfront. You’re paying for learning velocity, not proven returns on every tool.
Expect to be running multiple tools in the same category simultaneously. Different
teams will find different tools effective for different codebases and workflows.
Be prepared to build your own tooling where the market hasn’t caught up with your
needs. If you’re truly on the bleeding edge, commercial offerings may not exist yet for
some of your use cases.
Have a lightweight deprecation plan for every tool you adopt. At this pace, you’ll be
replacing things regularly as the landscape shifts. As such you always have to have a
planned off ramp before you start using a tool. How are you going to capture the
learnings and the context? What would be plausible approaches to replacing the tool
assuming they go out of business, get acquired in a way that doesn’t ensure the continuity you need or become irrelevant as the industry moves in another direction?

Hiring
Require candidates to use AI tools during the interview process. This isn’t a nice-tohave - it’s how you signal what kind of engineering org you are. Your rubrics need to
assess how effectively candidates collaborate with LLMs: decomposing problems,
providing context, validating output, and iterating on results.
Look for curiosity and adaptability over deep expertise with any single tool. The specific tools will change, but the mindset of working with AI won’t. Be honest in job
postings that this is an AI-forward org. You want to attract engineers who are excited
by that and filter out those who aren’t.
You will likely lose some experienced engineers who don’t want to work this way.
That’s a real loss - these are people with deep domain knowledge and institutional
context. But if you’re committed to this lane, you need to be honest about that tradeoff.

Setting the Tone
AI adoption is a mandate, not an invitation. This is how your engineering org works
now. Many good programmers do not want to become an engineering manager to a
bunch of agents. That is fine, but it’s not something you can allow within your org.
You need to make it clear that their job is to help to encode what “good software”
looks like at your company so agents can build quality software. As your software factory matures, they’ll then naturally gravitate towards a smaller platform team focused
on building and refining the pipelines, infrastructure and tooling to improve the
quality of your agentic code generation or towards the problem domain, writing
higher level specifications and validations to run experiments or meet customer
needs. They should stop writing code very soon, and understand they probably won’t
be reading it either in a few months.
Leaders also need to be visibly using AI tools themselves, not just endorsing them. If
you are not using a tool like claude code and capturing context to improve the quality
of your output while starting to build pipelines to automate management tasks and
flows, you’re not going to understand how to lead your organization.
Frame AI as the new baseline expectation, similar to how using version control or
CI/CD is simply expected. It’s not about “adopting AI,” it’s about how the team delivers software. Be transparent that short-term productivity may dip as people build new
skills. Set expectations with the board and the CEO that there will be an investment
period before the acceleration kicks in. Celebrate the engineers who push boundaries,
and make their experiments visible across the org.

Change Management and Enablement
In an org of 50 people or more, you need at least one dedicated person whose fulltime job is managing the logistics of AI adoption: training, comms, unblocking
teams, tracking experiments, and sharing what’s working. For most companies,
DevEx/Platform is a great place for this person to sit so they can act as a conduit
between the team building the software factories and the rest of the org that is tasked
with shipping reliable software without reading or writing the code. A background in
DevRel or a similar level of communication skills (forward deployed or solution engineer) works well. Someone who has history at the company and is respected by both
executives and ICs will be best positioned to lead the charge.
For smaller teams, someone is still going to have to focus on helping the team adopt
the new working practices. A little like “test infecting” engineers with TDD, “AI infection” is a good way to think about pairing someone who “gets” agentic development
with someone who is still trying to get up to speed. In the smallest teams maybe that
person is also heavily involved in building the orchestrator and context systems for
automating software development, bringing back what they learn from the feature
teams to improve the quality of the automations.

Weekly AI demos and monthly roundups should be standard rituals. You should also
add regular office hours (either in person or online) where people can ask questions
to learn from engineers who are more comfortable coding agentically. The pace of
change requires frequent touchpoints.
Invest in structured training–don’t just tell people to “watch some YouTube videos.”
Hold lunch-and-learns and paired sessions with strong AI practitioners, and dedicate

Build and maintain a shared context repository that includes documentation, prompt
libraries, experiment results, and proven patterns. This is how you compound learning across the org instead of having every team rediscover the same things.
Your platform/DevEx team should be driving the rollout of tools and practices to feature teams, treating AI enablement as a first-class product they’re delivering to their
internal customers. The job of a platform team is to enable and accelerate the feature
teams building the user valued software. For at least the next 6-18 months their most
important job will be to help feature teams use software factories to automate the
delivery of high value code.
Expect to revise your processes and rituals frequently. What works this month may
not work next month as the tools and your team’s capabilities evolve.

Early Adopter
As an early adopter, you’ll be able to take a little more time to let things settle, focusing on tools and practices that seem to be getting traction, but you are still going to
have to push hard on your team to identify, pilot and scale good practices quickly and
with the minimum of red tape and bureaucracy.

Tool Approval
You still need a fast approval process, but it can be slightly more structured than what
an innovator org would have. A week to review a new tool is reasonable; a month is
not.
You’re primarily licensing tools rather than building them, so your evaluation criteria
should focus on adoption trajectory, integration quality, and the vendor’s staying
power. As an early adopter you can’t wait for a definitive leader to emerge in every
category before adopting it. As such you should evaluate their mission and trajectory.
Just like you hire a junior engineer for potential, not just for their current skills or
track records, you should look for vendors that are thinking about agentic coding in a
way that’s consistent with your goals. You should also be thoughtful about whether
they are just one model release away from irrelevance - are they building a deep, differentiated moat that will continue to make them economically viable even as the
models from the foundation labs become more capable?

Look for engineers who are enthusiastic about AI and have been using it in their

Your job postings should make it clear that AI tools are part of your daily workflow.
Don’t be ambiguous about this - it’s a selling point for the candidates you want to

Setting the Tone
Provide strong encouragement to use AI tools, with a clear direction. This doesn’t
need to be a mandate, but it should come close. Set the expectation that everyone will
be using AI tools and building their proficiency. Leadership should be framing AI
adoption as a competitive advantage you’re actively investing in, not just an experiment.
Be clear that there’s organizational support for the learning curve, but also that the
direction is not optional. You’re heading here; the question is how quickly you can
bring everyone along. Share what you’re learning from the innovators ahead of you both their successes and their cautionary tales. It helps your team feel like the
approach is informed, not reckless.

Change Management and Enablement
Having a dedicated employee focus on enablement is still important, though it may be
a significant percentage of someone’s time rather than a full-time role, depending on
the organization’s size. Regular sharing rituals such as demos and roundups are valuable but can be slightly less frequent than in an innovator org: biweekly rather than
weekly might work.

Your training should be more structured and curated than an innovator org’s. You’re
rolling out proven patterns, not asking everyone to figure it out on their own. Identify internal champions who can support their teams in adopting tools and practices
that have already proven effective in your org. If you have a mature platform team,
they can be responsible for keeping up with good practices, and you’ll be moving at a
pace that lightweight but structured training materials (blog posts, simple training
sessions) will be more viable than at innovators where good practices may be changing weekly.
Document what’s working and create playbooks. You’re starting to move from “exploration” to “this is how we do things,” and that transition needs clear guidance.

Early Majority
In the early majority, you’re ceding the benefits from keeping up on the bleeding
edge, but in return, you can wait for good practices to emerge. The pace of change is
still going to be much faster than your team is used to, but in the early majority it’ll be
measured in quarters, not weeks or days.

Tool Approval
Create a formal review council or committee that evaluates tools once they’ve
emerged as leading players in their category. You’re not experimenting with unproven
tools, you’re adopting winners.
ROI expectations are reasonable here. You should be able to point to case studies and
proven value from earlier adopters before committing to a tool. It’s fair to look at
token costs and (other than a small budget for experimentation by your platform
team) to require a plausible ROI showing how the token burn will accelerate the
roadmap, improve quality or reduce headcount.
Standardize on a smaller set of approved tools rather than allowing teams to use
whatever they want. Minimize ad-hoc experimentation. The value at this stage comes
from disciplined, org-wide rollout of proven, consistent, supportable tools, not from
individual exploration.
Your risk team will be much more comfortable with this pace than they would be
with the innovator pace. Use that to your advantage. Get them to genuinely buy into
your experiments rather than just tolerating them.

Hiring
AI experience is a plus but not a requirement. You’re looking for engineers who are
open to using AI tools, not necessarily those who’ve been on the bleeding edge. Your
interview process should accommodate AI tools but doesn’t need to require them.
The signal you’re sending is “we’re adopting AI thoughtfully,” not “we’re all-in.”

Focus on strong engineering fundamentals. The tools you’ll be rolling out are more

Your tone should be supportive and encouraging, but measured. The message is:

Position AI adoption as part of a broader commitment to engineering excellence and

your CEO’s messaging is calibrated to this pace. Nothing undermines this lane faster

things out at an early-majority pace.

Change Management and Enablement
Your training can be more programmatic than exploratory. You’re teaching people
how to use specific approved tools and established workflows, not asking them to
experiment broadly. Group training sessions and structured onboarding for new tools
will be more effective than individual experimentation.
Focus on getting the fundamentals of your engineering org right–like CI/CD, test
coverage, and deployment automation–so you can actually absorb the acceleration
that AI tools provide.
Internal champions are still important, but their role is more about driving adoption
of approved tools than about discovering new approaches. Communication cadence
can be monthly rather than weekly. The pace of change is more manageable, and you
don’t want to create artificial urgency.

Tool Approval
Your existing vendor relationships are your primary channel for AI adoption. Wait
for your current tools (such as IDEs, CI/CD platforms, and cloud providers) to integrate AI capabilities natively.
A standard procurement process with standard timelines is fine. There’s no urgency

Avoid approving niche AI-specific tools. The overhead of managing additional vendors and integrations isn’t worth it when you can wait for your established vendors to
catch up–and they will, eventually. They’re even more motivated than you are,
because their early-adopter customers are asking hard questions.
Keep your red lines simple and conservative. You have the luxury of time, so use it to
avoid unnecessary risk.

Hiring
AI experience is not a meaningful part of your hiring rubrics. You’re hiring for the
same skills you always have, with the same interview process.
Don’t penalize candidates for using AI in interviews, but don’t specifically look for it
either.
Recognize that you probably won’t attract the most AI-forward engineers. You’ll
retain people who value stability and are comfortable with established practices,
which is consistent with this lane.
As AI capabilities become embedded in your standard tooling, provide enough training that your existing team can take advantage of the new features.

Setting the Tone
AI is available for those who want to use it, but it’s not a priority or an expectation.
The focus remains on your existing roadmap and your established ways of working.
Avoid dismissing AI as not worthwhile; that alienates the curious members of your
team. Instead, frame it as something you’re monitoring and will adopt as it matures
within the tools you already use.
Be honest with yourself and your team about the trade-offs. If your industry becomes
more AI-disrupted than you expected, you’ll need to accelerate, and your team
should understand that this lane isn’t permanent if conditions change.
Make sure the CEO and the board are genuinely aligned with this pace. If they’re
publicly claiming AI leadership while you’re operating as a laggard org, the inconsistency will damage the org’s credibility internally and externally.
